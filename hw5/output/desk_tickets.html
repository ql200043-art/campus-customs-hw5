Reflection — what the three tickets taught me

The final board looks simple: three resolved tickets, no pending proposals, and checking of 160.00. Getting there was less smooth. I would judge this system by whether its decisions were supported and its actions stayed within the rules, not just by whether every card turned green.

Evidence: the original Expected plans, final Actual sections and Cash tab on this page; resolved ticket records; the real audit trail; and the implemented MCP tools. This reflection evaluates existing runs; it does not run the team again.

1. How well did the agents perform on each ticket?

Ticket 101 — safe investigation, but an initially unclear finish line

The request was for one CC-TEE-WHITE in size S, with recorded stock of 0. Inventory correctly treated zero as a real stock shortage. The team used get_ticket, lookup_inventory, get_invoice and get_vendor_status to connect the request to invoice 501 and the vendor’s unpaid-invoice shipping blocker. Accounting checked cash and payment history and prepared the 840 invoice proposal. Liang Qiao’s explicit approval allowed the backend to execute payment 1, leaving 2560 from the starting 3400.

The strongest behavior came after payment: the agents did not treat a paid invoice as proof of received merchandise. The final post-payment investigation completed Inventory, Customer Service and Boss reports, but those reports still described blocked physical fulfillment. That was factually right. A recorded vendor lead time is not a shipment confirmation or a delivery date. The later evidence-checked operations-completion step closed the case with a truthful out-of-stock outcome and a draft-only customer response; it did not increase stock or verify replenishment, receipt, shipment or delivery.

I would rate the factual discipline highly and the initial workflow efficiency less highly. Repeated investigation in the first run reached the 24-MCP-call limit, and the prepared proposal needed recovery through the existing validation/staging path. We also had to clarify what “resolved” meant. The right finish line was completion of the supported operational work, not pretending the customer had received a tee.

Ticket 102 — a clear financial task, with excessive caution at first

Facilities verified lease 1 for the Chapel Street shop and Elm City Properties: rent of 2400 due 2026-09-02. Using get_shop_context, the team evaluated that date against desk.date_today, 2026-08-31, rather than the computer date. Accounting used list_cash_accounts and list_payments to establish checking of 2560 and no matching modeled rent payment. Paying the obligation would leave 160.

The first investigation unnecessarily asked whether rent might have been paid outside the database. That might matter in a real business with incomplete integrations, but it was an unsupported blocker in this simulation once the user clarified that the payments ledger was authoritative. In the follow-up run, Boss used prepare_payment to prepare the rent proposal. Anthony Qiao explicitly approved it, and the backend executed payment 2, updated the lease and closed the ticket transactionally. Checking fell from 2560 to 160. This was a successful financial outcome because approval and execution were separate from the agents’ recommendation, and no landlord contact was needed.

Ticket 103 — a supported “no” was the right result

Yale AI Club requested 20 CC-HOOD-NAVY hoodies in size M. Inventory found 8, a shortage of 12. Accounting verified unit cost 22 and list price 58 using lookup_pricing. The difference of 36 per unit supports arithmetic, not authority to give a bulk discount. Vendor checks also did not establish timely replenishment of this exact variant and quantity.

The final run used Inventory, Accounting and Customer Service under Boss. Customer Service drafted a factual decline, Boss recommended declining the request as stated, and the private MCP completion mechanism checked the evidence before closing the ticket. No payment occurred, checking stayed 160, and no stock, discount or shipment promise was invented. I consider that a legitimate resolution: the requested order was not accepted, but the investigation had reached a supported terminal decision.

This ticket also exposed the limits of the orchestration. An invalid delegation target was diagnosed in a reproduced UnexpectedModelBehavior failure; stricter tool-schema guidance addressed that observed failure. A later self-delegation/active-path-cycle block still stopped the workflow. The final guard records and skips a forbidden delegation without invoking a child, while keeping the delegation budget and other limits. The final successful run did not need a cycle. The lesson is to preserve the safety check while allowing a team with sufficient evidence to finish its decision.

The Expected sections are useful precisely because they were not rewritten to match the results. They show that I originally conflated an unresolved customer request with an unresolved operations case, and underestimated how much repeated delegation could cost.

3. What would have been simpler with one agent and tools?

One agent could have read a ticket, gathered the same MCP evidence, prepared a proposal or draft, and returned one structured report. That would remove inter-agent target validation, nested delegation paths, report handoffs and active-path cycle detection. It would also make debugging easier: there would be one conversation to trace instead of asking whether Boss, a specialist or a returned report caused the failure. It would not eliminate structured-output errors or the need for tool-call limits.

The five-agent design created overhead through repeated context loading, repeated factual lookups and additional model responses. Ticket 101’s tool-cap stop is concrete evidence that this matters. A single agent could reuse facts in one context more directly, although I cannot claim an exact token saving without a measured comparison. Both designs still need the same external payment approval, current-cash and duplicate checks, and transactional database protection.

The benefit of five roles was clearer responsibility. Facilities established the lease obligation; Accounting assessed funding; Inventory separated stock from vendor eligibility; Customer Service kept responses factual and draft-only; Boss assembled the final disposition. The audit makes these contributions attributable. That is helpful for review, but it also creates more events to interpret, and five agents using the same model are not five independent guarantees of correctness. A single agent could still be auditable if each tool call and decision were logged. For these three small tickets, I would favor simpler routing and shared evidence over deeper delegation. The actual successful workflows show that a flatter path can retain specialist accountability without unnecessary back-and-forth.

4. Three new problems the existing team could handle

These are hypothetical new requests, not new database records or claims that another customer has placed an order. “Handle” here means deliver the specified evidence-backed answer or draft; the current tools do not provide a general order-fulfillment service.

  1. An employee needs a factual stock-and-list-price reply for a specified SKU and size. Boss could ask Inventory to use lookup_inventory, Accounting to use lookup_pricing, and Customer Service to draft the answer from those results. This can answer recorded availability, location and list price, or clearly report a missing record. It cannot reserve the item or promise delivery. It is a useful service even without executing a sale.
  2. The manager wants to know whether a named recorded vendor has an invoice blocker before considering another order. Inventory could use list_vendors and get_vendor_status; Accounting could inspect the returned invoice IDs with get_invoice and matching payment history with list_payments. Boss could produce a blocker report using get_shop_context for due-date context. This answers whether an open unpaid invoice blocks shipping under the modeled rule. It does not assert that the vendor has the desired stock or place an order.
  3. The manager requests a lease-and-cash readiness briefing for an existing lease. Facilities could use lookup_lease and get_shop_context to identify the recorded obligation and timing. Accounting could use list_cash_accounts and list_payments to report current funding and recorded payment history; Boss would summarize any gap. At today’s final modeled checking balance, a 2400 obligation would exceed 160 by 2240, but that calculation is not a claim that another rent payment is currently owed. Ambiguous rent-period history must be reported rather than bypassed. The team can complete the briefing without creating or executing a payment.

5. Three new problems the current tools cannot fully solve

  1. Reorder the 12 missing medium hoodies and verify their arrival. The team can diagnose the shortage, but it lacks a verified vendor-to-SKU catalog, current supplier availability/quote, purchase-order records and receipt evidence. It would need approved purchase-order preparation/execution tools, order-status data and a narrowly controlled goods-receipt tool tied to verified quantities before inventory could increase. Inventory would manage the evidence and Accounting the approved funding. Vendor lead_days alone cannot supply these missing facts, and any external contact would require a separately authorized capability.
  2. Authorize and apply a bulk discount, then accept the club’s order. A pricing lookup provides cost and list price, not discount authority. This would require stored discount rules or an explicit human price-approval record, tools to prepare and approve a quote, and an order/reservation model with transaction-safe stock allocation. Accounting could assess margins, Boss could review the exception and Customer Service could draft the offer, but none can manufacture authority. Accepting and fulfilling an order goes beyond the current ticket-specific decline mechanism. Recording sales revenue would also require changing the homework’s cash-out-only scope.
  3. Process a return and refund for a customer’s previous purchase. There is no sale/payment-in record, return authorization, refund policy or verified inspection record to establish what was bought, what is owed, or whether returned stock is reusable. The invoice/rent payment tools are not refund tools. The system would need linked sales and return data, a refund-proposal tool, separate explicit human approval and duplicate-safe refund execution, plus an inspected-return inventory tool. Customer Service, Inventory and Accounting could share these responsibilities only after those capabilities and an appropriate business scope exist.

My main takeaway: The useful achievement was a controlled operational decision, not automatic agreement with every request. The two human-approved payments explain the full cash reconciliation: 3400 − 840 − 2400 = 160. Ticket 103 added no cash movement. All three cases are resolved, while the records remain honest about what was never fulfilled. Better completion definitions and bounded evidence sharing mattered as much as the number of agents.
